Banque du Liban

CENTRAL BANK OF LEBANON • FOUNDATION, GOVERNANCE & EVOLUTION

The Path to Central Banking (1930s – 1963)

The creation of the Banque du Liban (BDL) bridges political independence (1943) and ultimate monetary sovereignty[cite: 12]. Key intellectual milestones propelled its establishment:

AUB Institutionalists

Said Himadeh (Dean of Economic Studies) first called for a central bank in 1935 to regulate credit, smooth seasonal and cyclical price fluctuations, and manage monetary policy[cite: 7, 8]. Later scholars like Albert Badre and Salim Hoss advanced national accounting and central regulatory frameworks[cite: 7].

The 1953 Historic Petition

Spearheaded by historian Philip Hitti and published in major newspapers like al-Nahar and al-Hayat ahead of elections, a popular petition demanded a national central bank (*bayt al-mal al-Lubnani*) run by nationals without foreign interference[cite: 9, 10, 11].

Legislative Pillars

Pioneered by statesmen like Raymond Eddé (Bank Secrecy Law) and Pierre Eddé (Association of Banks in Lebanon), culminating in the promulgation of the Code of Money and Credit under Decree No. 13513 on August 1, 1963[cite: 13, 14].

Institutional Comparison & Legal Mandate

On April 1, 1964, BDL assumed full central banking authority, marking the definitive transition from the colonial-era Banque de Syrie et du Liban (BSL)[cite: 25].

Metric Banque de Syrie et du Liban (BSL) Banque du Liban (BDL)
Establishment Evolved from BSGL; convention adopted in 1937[cite: 6]. Established on August 1, 1963; operational April 1, 1964[cite: 1, 6].
Nature Private institution[cite: 6]. Public, state-owned central bank with full financial autonomy[cite: 6, 17].
Jurisdiction & Role Operated across both Lebanon and Syria with exclusive currency rights[cite: 6]. Operates solely within Lebanon, issuing the Lebanese Pound and directing monetary policy[cite: 6, 15].
Governance Private oversight cooperating with governments[cite: 6]. Governed by the Code of Money and Credit under government supervision, exempt from public sector administrative rules[cite: 6, 17].

Article 70 Mission

BDL's overarching duty is safeguarding the currency as a foundation for economic development, price stability, and banking system soundness[cite: 24].

European Technical Aid

Staff training and administrative structures were built with expert support from the Banque de France and the National Bank of Belgium[cite: 26].

Early Crises & Supervisory Institutions

The transition period was marred by severe banking failures that exposed early structural weaknesses and catalyzed vital regulatory reforms[cite: 31, 35].

Event / Bank Date Context & BDL Action Systemic Impact
Real Estate Bank Feb – Mar 1964 Occurred during BSL-to-BDL handover. BDL deferred to BSL; court declared bankruptcy on March 12, 1964[cite: 31, 32]. Exposed BDL's initial institutional weakness and lack of crisis mechanisms[cite: 31].
Sogex Bank January 1965 Post-collapse panic. BDL organized a private-sector-led liquidation via 10 commercial banks without direct public cash intervention[cite: 33]. Contained immediate panic, though BDL's annual report acknowledged it as a one-time remedy[cite: 33].
Intra Bank Crisis Fall 1966 Lebanon's largest bank collapsed after years of unchecked expansion and ignored BDL warnings, severely damaging BDL's early legitimacy[cite: 35]. Triggered sweeping legislative reforms and a complete overhaul of state-banking relations[cite: 35].

Post-Crisis Regulatory Bodies

Banking Control Commission (BCC)

Established by Law 28/67 with extraordinary enforcement powers over bank inspections and compliance[cite: 37].

Higher Banking Commission (HBC)

Created to oversee bank mergers, disciplinary measures, and sanctions[cite: 36].

National Deposit Insurance Corp. (NDIC)

Formed with a mixed public-private structure to insure bank deposits and restore public trust[cite: 36].

Historical Governors & Ad Interim Leadership

The leadership of BDL has navigated decades of prosperity, regional conflict, and severe financial restructuring[cite: 38, 41, 43, 44, 47, 50, 51].

Governor Name Term in Office Key Achievements & Historical Context
Philippe Takla 1963 – 1967 Inaugural Governor; laid foundational structures and navigated the Intra Bank crisis[cite: 38].
Joseph Oughourlian (Ad Interim) 1976 – 1978 First Sub-Governor (1964–1983); de facto head under Takla and steered policy during early civil war years[cite: 51].
Elias Sarkis 1967 – 1976 Bolstered gold reserves, established the BCC and HBC, later served as President of Lebanon[cite: 40].
Michel el-Khoury 1978–1985 / 1991–1993 Served two distinct terms; navigated the 1982 Israeli invasion and initial LBP currency collapse[cite: 42].
Edmond Naïm 1985 – 1991 Faced severe currency devaluation, plummeting deposits, and soaring dollarization during intense conflict[cite: 44].
Riad Salamé 1993 – 2023 Longest-serving governor (30 years); oversaw long stability followed by the 2019 financial meltdown[cite: 47].
Wassim Mansouri (Ad Interim) 2023 – 2025 Halted state deficit financing, eliminated subsidies, and rebuilt foreign currency reserves to $10.746B[cite: 51].
Karim A. Souaid March 2025 – Present Current Governor tasked with banking sector restructuring, depositor resolution, and IMF economic recovery engagement[cite: 50].
